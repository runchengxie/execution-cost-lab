An interactive execution study

Why large orders go unfinished

Market volume, available cash, and time each set a different limit. Three synthetic examples show what happens when a trading plan meets those limits.

Cash on the ledger, cash you can use

A sale is booked and three buy orders share one account. Change when the proceeds become usable and how the account divides its budget.

With cash constrained, both allocation rules buy the same total number of shares. The position shortfall can be concentrated in one order or spread across all three.

Bigger orders need more liquidity

At a given participation rate, only a fraction of market volume is available to the simulated order. Change order size or participation to compare completion over time.

This example treats a share of observed volume as an execution budget. Actual fills depend on prices, queue position, order limits and the market’s response.

Cost can rise when you fill more

A longer schedule completes more of the order. Compare all fills, then match share counts to see the difference in the cost of execution.

Execution cost combines a fee and a price difference. Opportunity cost values unfilled shares at a common terminal price. It appears separately and may be negative.

What is being measured?

All inputs are invented for teaching. Completion is filled shares divided by requested shares. The budget model assumes 6 basis points in fees for the cost example. Price differences and terminal movement are scenario inputs. No real-order or market-impact estimate is made.
